L'Union Fait La Force

Overview

Project Description

This research argues that participatory approaches are key to achieving equality in natural language processing (NLP). There is a large disparity between languages in available NLP resources, and low-resource languages — like Haitian Creole, my family's native language — are consistently underserved. The central claim: to close this gap, we must actively involve low-resource language communities in developing NLP tools, extending their involvement beyond the typical roles of human feedback and data collection.

The title borrows Haiti's national motto, "L'union fait la force" — "unity is strength," or colloquially "together we are strong." It captures the paper's thesis: communities, not just data, sustain a language. This work was advised by Professor Harini Suresh, with sources from Brown University's SOLAR lab. The full paper is available as a PDF.

Research Goal

  • Develop methods to improve linguistic diversity in NLP resources
  • Analyze how people create linguistic resources for low-resource languages
  • Demonstrate that participatory principles are necessary for NLP equality

Literature Review

Frameworks

The analysis builds on a lineage of participatory-research scholarship, adapted to the NLP setting:

  • A Ladder of Participation [Arnstein 1969] — the foundational text of participatory research, arguing that marginalized groups must hold real decision-making power to achieve equality.
  • The State and Fate of Linguistic Diversity [Joshi et al. 2020] — categorizes languages by NLP-resource availability and calls on researchers to address the disparity.
  • The Participatory Turn in AI Design [Delgado et al. 2023] — the core framework, transforming the Ladder of Participation into the Parameters of Participation for AI/ML systems.
  • Data sovereignty — drawn from Te Hiku Media [Hao 2022] and Masakhane [Masakhane 2024]: a community's right to decide how its data are collected and used.

Case Studies

Sources range from projects that deeply involve native speakers at every step — data collection, algorithm development, benchmark design, and evaluation — to projects that rely on traditional methods like fine-tuning with no community input.

Methodology

I adapt Delgado et al.'s Parameters of Participation into The NLP Participatory Matrix. Each project is assessed across three dimensions — the goal (why is participation needed?), the scope (who is involved?), and the form (what shape does participation take?). Building on Suresh et al. [2024], I score each along five modes of participation, adding a fifth mode — no participation — for projects with little to no community involvement.

Modes of Participation

  1. No participation — no community involvement
  2. Consultation — eliciting user preferences
  3. Inclusion — deliberation around specific design choices
  4. Collaboration — co-creation of design possibilities
  5. Ownership — stakeholders shape the entire design process

Case Study Analysis

  • No participation (Tweeties, LexC-Gen) — neither project orients its goals toward local communities; they prioritize working with limited data over community involvement.
  • Consultation (Cendol) — works with NLP experts and non-experts, but only to gather feedback and linguistic nuance.
  • Inclusion (AfriCOMET) — native speakers help collect and evaluate data, but do not control project design such as the evaluation metrics.
  • Collaboration (Masakhane) — local communities collect, translate, and evaluate data and share ownership of data and models with the organization.
  • Ownership (Te Hiku) — the Māori community has the final say over the project's direction and data access.

Discussion

Projects with higher levels of involvement — collaboration and ownership — tend to perform better than those with low participation. Low-participation projects run into recurring problems:

  • Cultural bias: NLP models carry over cultural biases from high-resource languages [Cahyawijaya et al. 2024; Remy et al. 2024].
  • Mixed results: some languages show no improvement in MT quality compared to other evaluation metrics [Wang et al. 2023].

I theorize this is because (1) having native speakers create and evaluate the data removes high-resource-language biases, and (2) involving people from numerous disciplines introduces new perspectives and ideas. Overall, the higher the mode of participation, the more robust the project.

Recommendations

If you research low-resource languages, incorporate collaboration or ownership. Without fully including the language's community, you risk omitting essential linguistic and cultural aspects from your NLP application. The two modes are executed similarly — the key difference is that under ownership, the language communities hold more decision-making power than the initial researchers. Apply participation across every stage:

  • Goal setting — communities help define goals, outcomes, and intended impact
  • Researcher selection — communities have a central role in choosing the research team
  • Data collection — communities determine what data is collected, how, and by whom
  • Model development — communities decide which models are used and run training and testing
  • Evaluation — communities design the metrics and judge result quality
  • Project deployment — communities govern how and where the project is deployed
  • Data access — shared ownership of datasets, models, and results

Language is so much more than just words; it is created and sustained by communities, which must be accounted for.
